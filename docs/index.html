Abundant alexandrite deposits were first discovered in 1830 in Russia’s Ural Mountains

GEM HISTORY: Abundant alexandrite deposits were first discovered in 1830 in Russia’s Ural Mountains. Those first alexandrites were of very fine quality and displayed vivid hues and dramatic color change. The gem was named after the young Alexander II, heir apparent to the throne. It caught the country’s attention because its red and green colors mirrored the national military colors of imperial Russia.

GEMOLOGY FACT: Tanzanite is relatively new to the colored stone galaxy. He alerted a local fortune hunter named Manuel d'Souza, who quickly registered four mining claims.

GEM HISTORY: The name for imperial topaz originated in nineteenth-century Russia. At the time, the Ural Mountains were topaz’s leading source, and the pink gemstone mined there was named to honor the Russian czar. Ownership of the gem was restricted to the royal family.

How a novel helped create opal’s unlucky reputation

GEM HISTORY: Opal is considered an October birthstone. Some people think it’s unlucky for anyone born in another month to wear an opal. But that particular superstition comes from a novel written in the 1800s ( Anne of Geierstein by Sir Walter Scott), and not from any ancient belief or experience. In fact, throughout most of history, opal has been regarded as the luckiest and most magical of all gems because it can show all colors. Once, it was thought to have the power to preserve the life and color of blond hair.

Pearl nacre contains plate-like aragonite crystals

GEMOLOGY FACT: Nacre is built from plate-like aragonite crystals, a form of calcium carbonate, held together by organic material. Light interacts with the pearl's surface and concentric nacre layers to produce luster. Nacre is therefore part of the structure behind a pearl's appearance, rather than simply a color description. Its condition matters alongside shape, surface and matching in jewelry.

Pearls need gentle care around chemicals

GEMOLOGY FACT: Pearl material can be damaged by acids and everyday chemicals, including perfume, cosmetics and perspiration. Its softness also makes the surface vulnerable to scratching. GIA advises against steam and ultrasonic cleaning and recommends a very soft, clean cloth after wearing. Gentle care helps preserve the nacre surface responsible for a pearl's appearance.

Ruby and sapphire both belong to the corundum family

GEMOLOGY FACT: Ruby and sapphire are varieties of the same mineral species, corundum. Red gem corundum is called ruby; sapphire includes blue and many other colors. Chromium produces ruby's red, while different trace elements contribute to other corundum colors. The shared mineral identity explains the relationship, but does not make the varieties interchangeable in a gem description.

Copper contributes to the blue of turquoise

GEMOLOGY FACT: Turquoise is a hydrated copper-aluminum phosphate. It forms where copper-rich water reacts with minerals containing aluminum and phosphorus. Its colors range from blue to green, and remnants of the surrounding rock can form dark veins called matrix. Both color and matrix influence appearance, so two pieces of genuine turquoise can look quite different.

GIA and the Gübelin Gem Lab independently confirmed that Ethiopia is a new source of copper-bearing tourmaline that meets the widely accepted definition of Paraíba tourmaline. Gübelin assembled approximately 300 rough and faceted reference stones, while GIA examined a parcel of more than 100 samples. GIA states that it can now confidently identify Ethiopia as an origin on Tourmaline Identification and Origin Reports. This does not mean every Ethiopian tourmaline is Paraíba; individual stones still require proper gemological testing and must meet accepted Paraíba criteria.
